Why Managers Struggle to Give Fair Performance Reviews

June 2, 2026 • 7 min read

Most managers want to conduct fair and accurate performance reviews.

However, fairness becomes difficult when managers are asked to evaluate an employee's contribution across an entire year based on limited information, fragmented feedback, and memory.

The problem is not a lack of intent.

The problem is the process.

Traditional performance appraisal systems place a significant burden on managers by expecting them to reconstruct months of employee performance during review season.

As organizations grow, this challenge becomes even more difficult.

The Impossible Task Managers Face

Consider a manager responsible for:

  • 10 team members
  • Multiple projects
  • Ongoing deliverables
  • Client interactions
  • Operational responsibilities

At the end of the year, that manager is expected to accurately answer questions such as:

  • Who consistently delivered quality work?
  • Who contributed beyond expectations?
  • Who improved throughout the year?
  • Who struggled with execution?
  • How did each employee perform across different projects?

Without structured performance data, these answers often rely on memory and perception.

That creates risk.

Human Memory Is Not Designed for Annual Evaluations

People naturally remember:

  • Recent events
  • Significant successes
  • Major failures
  • Highly visible contributions

They do not naturally remember:

  • Daily task execution
  • Consistent performers
  • Small but important contributions
  • Work completed months earlier

This creates gaps in performance reviews.

Employees who contribute steadily throughout the year may receive less recognition than employees whose work was simply more visible or more recent.

Team Changes Create Additional Challenges

In many organizations:

  • Managers change teams
  • Employees move between projects
  • Team leaders are reassigned
  • Organizational structures evolve

When a manager joins midway through the year, they often lack context about earlier contributions.

As a result:

  • Historical performance becomes difficult to assess
  • Ratings depend on incomplete information
  • Appraisal consistency decreases

The system expects managers to evaluate work they may never have directly observed.

Why Employees Lose Trust in Appraisals

When employees believe evaluations are subjective, trust begins to erode.

Common employee concerns include:

  • "My work wasn't recognized."
  • "Only recent projects were discussed."
  • "My manager wasn't involved in earlier assignments."
  • "The rating doesn't reflect my overall contribution."

Whether these concerns are accurate or not, perception matters.

A performance review process that feels unfair can negatively affect:

  • Engagement
  • Morale
  • Retention
  • Motivation

Employees want transparency and evidence behind performance decisions.

The Hidden Cost of Traditional Appraisal Cycles

Performance reviews are also expensive from an operational perspective.

Managers spend significant time:

  • Collecting feedback
  • Reviewing historical work
  • Comparing employees
  • Preparing documentation
  • Attending review meetings

HR teams spend weeks coordinating appraisal cycles across the organization.

The larger the workforce, the greater the administrative burden.

This effort is repeated every review cycle.

Continuous Performance Evaluation Changes the Process

Instead of evaluating performance months after work is completed, organizations can capture performance information continuously.

This approach records:

  • Task execution
  • Contribution quality
  • Timesheet activity
  • Project involvement
  • Structured feedback

While work is happening.

Rather than reconstructing performance history, organizations build it continuously.

This creates a more reliable foundation for appraisals.
